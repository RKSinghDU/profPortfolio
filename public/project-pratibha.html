The premise

Same company. New chair. New instrument.

Pratibha — प्रतिभा — means talent and potential. Your mandate is to build a performance-management system that surfaces it, develops it and keeps it. You already know this organisation. What changes is your chair, your instrument — and the evidence in front of you.

What changed about the job

Consultant → Business Partner

An OD consultant hands over a report. A Business Partner lives inside the consequence. Every rupee, every hour and every unit of goodwill you spend this semester is gone for the rest of it, and the organisation you hand back in Week 12 is the one you built.

What changed about the tooling

Spreadsheet → portal

Last semester the simulation ran on Apps Script and a Google Sheet. Pratibha runs on a portal with live resource tiles, a Cost Calculator that prices an option before you commit, an option Playbook, a running decision ledger and an organisation scoreboard. The analysis is still yours; the bookkeeping is not.

What changed about the evidence

A different file, for a different question

Do not reach for your OD workbook. That was a 5,000-row file built to answer an organisational-development question. Pratibha issues a new 2,000-row dataset built to answer a performance-management one — different fields, different structure, and its own data dictionary. Read the dictionary before you compute anything. Any figure carried across from last semester is a figure you cannot defend.

You compute before you are briefed

Each round opens with compute tasks, not a case. You work that round's figures out of the raw 2,000-row dataset first. Only then do you read the brief — which either confirms what you found or deliberately contradicts it.

Nothing resets between rounds

A weak diagnosis in Round 1 becomes a mis-built system in Round 2 and an expensive correction in Round 4. The simulation rewards diagnostic depth and resource discipline; it punishes superficial reading and intervention spam.

Same option, different marks

Two teams can select identically and score very differently. What is graded is the quality of the reasoning — the citations, the alternatives you weighed, the trade-off you named out loud.

A word on the unexpected

Real HR does not run to plan

Somewhere in this course the ground will shift under you, and no reading will have pre-scripted the answer. That is deliberate, and it is the most authentic thing the simulation will ask of you. When it happens, do what strong HR leaders do: slow down, diagnose the real problem beneath the noise, protect people and trust first, and reason freshly rather than reaching for last round's plan. Keep some budget, some capacity and some goodwill in reserve — a plan with no slack in it is not a plan.

Four resources, four behaviours

You cannot buy your way through this

The hardest part is not that resources are scarce. It is that they are scarce in four different ways, and a plan that respects one usually spends another.

HR Budget

Spends down

Hard cash for the whole year across all six rounds. Overspend early and the later rounds starve — including the one where you most need room.

HR Capacity

40 hrs / round
Resets

Analytical and delivery capacity. Refills every round and cannot exceed 40 in any single one, so you cannot stack interventions — only sequence them.

Manager Cooperation

55 / 100
Persists

Built by consulting and training; lost by imposing and surveilling. Below about 30, interventions get harder. Above about 80, advanced options unlock.

Employee Readiness

68 / 100
Persists

Built by piloting, communicating and listening; lost by surprise rollouts and broken promises. Ask people what they think and then do nothing, and it falls further than if you had never asked.

And a fifth thing you cannot see

The hidden layer

Five cultural dimensions — trust and psychological safety among them — move quietly with every decision you take. You see them as bands, never numbers, exactly as a real HR leader would. You cannot optimise what you cannot measure precisely, and that is the point.

Five lenses

You do not choose your seat

Ten teams of five. Within each team everyone holds one of five persona lenses — a standing professional job, not a chore rota. The platform assigns them; you do not choose. They rotate every round on a fixed roster so that every member holds every lens, and Round 6 returns to the Round 1 arrangement. You see your past and current rounds only; future rotations stay hidden.

Your private layer

One decision per team should not erase four other minds

Alongside the team's single binding decision, three things belong to you alone. None of them is graded. None of them touches the team's resources, marks or record.

The moment your team submits

Shadow Ballot

The option you would have chosen, at least twenty words on what that reveals about your HR instinct, and whether you supported the team's pick, had reservations, or argued against it. If you disagreed, this is where your view survives. File it the same day — the window closes.

From Week 2, every round

Data Twin

In Week 2 you are matched to one real row in the Bharat Textiles dataset — an employee whose aspirations resemble your own. After each decision you privately note what your team just did to them. Never collected, never seen.

Weeks 4, 8 and 11

Reflection Logs

Three pieces of 300–500 words, written in the portal against a prompt shown there. They feed your individual engagement mark and, together with the ballots, they are what your HR Kundali is read from.

A second kind of learning

The team is marked.
You are read.

Everything above is the call you learn to make as a team. Your HR Kundali is the other half — a chart of your own instincts, and a rising sign for the kind of HR professional you are becoming.

Its spine is the Shadow Ballot you file after every round — the option you would personally have chosen, and why. Onto that spine it sets how your teammates saw you in the peer evaluations, and your three reflection logs.

From those it reads six instincts, and names two of them: the one you lead with — your archetype — and the one you lean on least, your blindspot.

01 · DIAGNOSTICIAN

Evidence-first

Establish what is actually true before anybody acts.

02 · HUMANIST

03 · ARCHITECT

Private, and never graded

Your Shadow Ballots are seen by you and by me, and by nobody else — not your teammates, not the class. They move no marks, no resources and no scores, and the Kundali itself carries no grade at all.

A blend, not a label

Most people run two or three instincts strongly. The chart names a leading tendency and a neglected one; it does not sort you into a type, and nothing about it is fixed. The blindspot is the useful half.

You cannot game it

The window opens the moment your team submits and closes sixty minutes later, precisely so you answer with your real in-the-room reaction rather than something composed afterwards. Answer honestly and the chart is worth having.

Your private record comes back to you at the end of the specialisation as a keepsake of your year as an HR Business Partner — after every mark is in, so that nothing in it can be mistaken for an assessment.

Six ways teams lose marks

All of these are avoidable, and all of them recur

A justification with no numbers

This scores zero on analytical rigour however well it is written. Cite the round's mandatory figures every time.

Reading the brief before doing the compute task

You will anchor on the brief's framing and stop thinking. The COMPUTE TASKS box goes up on Classroom first for exactly this reason.

Spending big in Round 2

Round 2 is the largest build and the most tempting. Teams that exhaust the budget there play the last three rounds on leftovers. Do the ₹180L arithmetic in Round 2, not Round 5.

Claiming your option has no downside

Every option trades something away. Naming it is a mark of judgement; hiding it reads as selling.

Losing the thread

Round 6 grades coherence across the whole year. A decision that ignores your own earlier findings will be pressed in the debrief.

Submitting at the last minute

Submit is irreversible and the page locks immediately. Only the Manager Advocate can press it. If yours cannot make a deadline, tell me before it — a stand-in can be authorised for one round, but there is no retrospective fix.

If you are stuck

Four things that are not your mistake

There is no turnover column in the dataset

Correct — there isn't one. Turnover is given to you by the case, not computed. The file contains an exit-risk score, which is a prediction, not a departure. Never substitute one for the other.

Our figures differ from another team's

Usually rounding or filtering. State your method in the justification: a defended method beats a copied number.

The balance did not move after we submitted

Wait a minute, then hard-refresh with Ctrl+Shift+R. And remember Round 1 moves nothing on the scoreboard by design.

Our team cannot agree

Good — argue it properly. One option gets submitted; your own view goes in your Shadow Ballot, which is exactly what it is for.
