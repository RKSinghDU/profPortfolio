L1 · What a generation is

Mannheim: a generation is more than a birth year

Generation location

Born in the same period. Necessary, never sufficient: it is only a potential.

Generation as actuality

Exposure to the same formative events in youth creates a shared stratum of experience.

Generation units

Groups inside one generation respond to the same events in different, even opposed, ways.

At Bharat Textiles

A Gen Z label borrowed from US usage assumes formative events a Bhiwandi loom operator and a Bengaluru coder never shared.

Mannheim, K. (1952). The problem of generations. In Essays on the Sociology of Knowledge. Routledge and Kegan Paul (original 1928)

L1 · The identification problem

Age, period and cohort cannot all be separated

The identity

Cohort = period minus age

Know any two and the third is fixed. No design can estimate all three effects without an untestable assumption.

DesignHolds constantConfounds
One survey, one yearPeriodAge with cohort
Time lag: same age, different yearsAgeCohort with period
Longitudinal: same people over yearsCohortAge with period

Costanza et al. (2012) and Twenge et al. (2010) both state this limit of single time point designs

L1 · Reading Costanza closely

Costanza et al. (2012): what lies beneath near zero

The claim being tested

A generation differs because of shared formative experience, and not only because of age. The meta analysis tests that definition, not the labels.

The pattern, not the headline

No difference in job satisfaction. Boomers somewhat more committed. Millennials moderately more likely to intend to leave. Gen X against Millennials depends on the form of commitment.

The authors' reading

Differences that do appear are more likely due to factors other than generational membership, such as age and career stage.

The two findings to remember

Studies disagree on where each generation begins and ends. Targeted generational interventions may therefore not be effective.

Costanza, Badger, Fraser, Severt and Gade (2012), Journal of Business and Psychology, 27(4), 375 to 394

07

L1 · Reading Twenge closely

Twenge et al. (2010): what was really measured

What was measured

Values about an ideal job, reported at 18, before full time work. Anticipated preferences, not experience of a job.

The theory behind it

Cultural change toward individualism, the Generation Me thesis: each cohort absorbs the culture of its youth.

The pattern

Leisure up and work centrality down. Extrinsic values peaked with Gen X. Intrinsic and social values lower. No rise in altruism, against the press.

The limits

Cohort is confounded with period. Values at 18 may shift at work. One US dataset. The Generation Me thesis is contested.

Twenge, Campbell, Hoffman and Lance (2010), Journal of Management, 36(5), 1117 to 1142; Trzesniewski and Donnellan (2010), Perspectives on Psychological Science, 5(1)

L1 · The rival theory

The lifespan developmental alternative

Proposition

Development continues across the whole working life and is shaped by context, not fixed by birth year.

Mechanism

People change with age, experience, health, family stage and the time they see ahead of them.

Critique of generations

Generational accounts name groups but supply no mechanism linking birth year to behavior at work.

At Bharat Textiles

Ask what changed in BT-2026400 across 22.5 years, not which band he was born into.

L1 · Unpacking age

Kooij et al. (2008): five meanings of age

Chronological

Calendar age

Functional

Performance, health, cognitive ability

Psychosocial

How old one feels and is seen to be

Organizational

Tenure, career stage, role age

Lifespan

Life events, family stage

Each meaning predicts different motives. A generational label collapses all five into the first.

Akkermans et al. (2016): once subjective age is measured, chronological age adds almost nothing to work motivation.

Kooij, de Lange, Jansen and Dikkers (2008), Journal of Managerial Psychology, 23(4), 364 to 394

L1 · A mechanism for age

Socioemotional selectivity theory

Time seen as open ended

Goals favor knowledge, novelty, new contacts and future payoff

Growth, mobility, learning

Time seen as limited

Goals favor emotionally meaningful relationships and present meaning

Depth, mastery, generativity

The driver is perceived future time, not age itself. A layoff threat or an illness can shorten it at 30.

Carstensen, L. L., Isaacowitz, D. M., and Charles, S. T. (1999). Taking time seriously. American Psychologist, 54(3), 165 to 181

L1 · How people manage change across the lifespan

Selection, optimization and compensation

Selection

Narrow goals to a few that matter most, by choice or after a loss

Optimization

Invest time and effort to refine the means for those goals

Compensation

Find substitute means when a capacity declines

Design implication: let experienced employees select and deepen, rather than push them toward breadth they have chosen to give up.

Baltes, P. B., and Baltes, M. M. (1990). Successful Aging: Perspectives from the Behavioral Sciences. Cambridge University Press, 1 to 34

L1 · Why labels cause harm

Stereotypes and the ecological fallacy

Ecological fallacy

Inferring an individual's attributes from a group correlation. A group average is not a fact about each member.

Robinson (1950)

Age stereotypes at work

Older workers seen as poorer performers, resistant to change, slower to learn, costlier. Evidence supports few of these.

Posthuma and Campion (2009)

A generational program institutionalizes both errors: it assigns a group average to every member, and it acts on beliefs the evidence does not support.

Robinson (1950), American Sociological Review, 15(3), 351 to 357; Posthuma and Campion (2009), Journal of Management, 35(1), 158 to 188

L2 · What onboarding must produce

Newcomer adjustment: three proximal outcomes

Role clarity

Knowing what is expected and how performance is judged

Self efficacy

Confidence in one's ability to do the job

Social acceptance

Feeling liked, trusted and included by insiders

These mediate between socialization and distal outcomes: performance, satisfaction, commitment, intention to remain. Each is delivered mainly by the manager.

Bauer, Bodner, Erdogan, Truxillo and Tucker (2007). Journal of Applied Psychology, 92(3), 707 to 721

L2 · Why people leave

The unfolding model: most exits begin with a shock

PathShock?Process
1YesShock triggers a script already in mind; leave without weighing alternatives
2YesShock violates one's image of self or values; leave, often without another job
3YesShock prompts a search; leave for a better alternative
4NoSatisfaction erodes gradually; leave with or without searching

An annual engagement survey can see only path 4. Paths 1 to 3 start at a moment.

L2 · The central debate

Mutual gains or conflicting outcomes?

Mutual gains

Good HR practice raises performance and wellbeing together, through commitment and reciprocity.

Social exchange logic

Conflicting outcomes

Practices raise performance by intensifying work; wellbeing pays the price.

Labor process critique

Van De Voorde et al. (2012): happiness and relationship wellbeing fit mutual gains; health wellbeing fits conflicting outcomes.

Peccei and Van De Voorde (2019): across 46 studies, mutual gains dominates how research is framed; rival models are rarely tested.

Van De Voorde, Paauwe and Van Veldhoven (2012), International Journal of Management Reviews, 14(4), 391 to 407; Peccei and Van De Voorde (2019), Human Resource Management Journal, 29(4)

L2 · How depletion happens

Conservation of resources and recovery

Conservation of resources

People strive to obtain and protect resources. Loss weighs more than equal gain.

Those with fewer resources lose more and invest defensively: loss spirals.

Hobfoll (1989)

Recovery experiences

Psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery, control over leisure.

Strain becomes burnout when recovery fails, not when effort is high.

Sonnentag and Fritz (2007)

A wellbeing program adds a resource. If demands keep draining faster, the spiral continues.

Hobfoll (1989), American Psychologist, 44(3), 513 to 524; Sonnentag and Fritz (2007), Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, 12(3), 204 to 221

L3 · The case for differentiation

Exclusive talent management

Proposition

Some positions contribute disproportionately to strategy; invest disproportionately in the people who fill them.

Architecture

Identify pivotal positions, build a talent pool of high potential and high performing people, and differentiate HR to fit.

Critique

Differentiation signals to the excluded majority that they matter less, with justice and morale costs.

At Bharat Textiles

Which roles are pivotal? Collings and Mellahi start from positions, not from the people rated highest.

Collings, D. G., and Mellahi, K. (2009). Strategic talent management: A review and research agenda. Human Resource Management Review, 19(4), 304 to 313

33

L3 · Individual differentiation

Idiosyncratic deals

Definition

Voluntary, personalized terms of a nonstandard nature, negotiated between an individual and the employer, benefiting both.

Content and timing

Flexibility, development or task content; negotiated at hiring (ex ante) or later (ex post).

Not favoritism

An i deal is open, tied to contribution and mutually beneficial; favoritism is none of these.

The fairness condition

Coworkers accept i deals when the process is transparent and the same chance is open to them.

Rousseau, D. M., Ho, V. T., and Greenberg, J. (2006). I deals: Idiosyncratic terms in employment relationships. Academy of Management Review, 31(4), 977 to 994
