L1 · Where the strengths idea comes from

Three traditions, one shared claim

Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi (2000)

Positive psychology

Psychology had studied repair of damage far more than the conditions in which people flourish. Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi (2000) proposed rebalancing the science.

Cameron, Dutton and Quinn (2003)

Positive organizational scholarship

The same rebalancing applied to organizations: study what generates exceptional performance, not only what causes failure.

Clifton and Harter (2003)

Strengths based development

Clifton's Gallup program: a strength is talent multiplied by investment. Develop what is already strong rather than chase what is weak.

Shared claim: the highest returns come from building on what is already present. Our task is to test that claim, not to adopt it.

L1 · The evidence that disturbs the commit question

Kluger and DeNisi (1996): feedback intervention theory

What they pooled

607 effect sizes from 131 papers, covering 23,663 observations of feedback on performance.

The average

A positive average effect, d = 0.41. Feedback helps on average.

The finding that matters

In over one third of cases, feedback reduced performance. The average hides a large harmful tail.

Why it happens

Feedback moves attention. Toward the task, performance rises. Toward the self, performance falls.

Kluger, A. N., and DeNisi, A. (1996). Psychological Bulletin, 119(2), 254 to 284

07

L1 · The mechanism

Where feedback sends a person's attention

Level of attentionWhat the person thinks aboutEffect on performance
Task learningThe details of how to do the taskCan help or hurt; detail can overload
Task motivationThe gap between goal and current performanceUsually helps; this is goal setting at work
Meta task (the self)What this says about me, my standing, my worthUsually hurts; effort goes to protecting the self
A weakness label, a forced rank or a public comparison pulls attention up to the self. The critique of annual appraisal is a theory, not a mood.

Kluger and DeNisi (1996); Locke and Latham (2002)

L1 · A design built on that theory

The feedforward interview and strengths based appraisal

1

Story of success

The employee describes an episode at work when they felt at their best and performed well.

2

Conditions

Together, name the conditions that made it possible: their actions, the manager's actions, the setting.

3

Feedforward

How close is your current work to those conditions? What would bring the next quarter closer?

4

Goals from the gap

Goals are set from that distance, so attention stays on task motivation, not on the self.

Kluger and Nir (2010), Human Resource Management Review, 20(3); Bouskila Yam and Kluger (2011), Human Resource Management Review, 21(2)

L1 · How strong is the evidence?

Read the strengths literature as you read Costanza

van Woerkom and Meyers (2015)

What is supported

Perceived organizational support for strengths use relates to positive affect and in role performance.

Ghielen, van Woerkom and Meyers (2018)

What reviews conclude

Strengths interventions show benefits for wellbeing and engagement; performance effects are less consistent.

Design and source

Where to be careful

Much evidence comes from the vendor that sells the assessment, and few studies use controlled designs.

Ask of any strengths claim: who measured it, against what comparison group, and was the outcome performance or feeling good?

L1 · The critique

Strengths can be overused

Grant and Schwartz (2011)

Too much of a good thing

Many traits follow an inverted U: beyond a point, more of a strength harms performance. Recall Warr's vitamins from Week 8.

Kaiser and Overfield (2011)

Lopsided leadership

Managers rated highest on one side, such as forceful, were often rated as overdoing it; strengths and overuse travel together.

Aguinis, Gottfredson and Joo (2012)

Weakness that matters

When a gap blocks the job, it must be addressed. Strengths first is a sequence, not an amnesty.

L2 · Why firms build from inside

Internal labor markets and human capital

Doeringer and Piore (1971)

Internal labor markets

Hiring happens at a few ports of entry; jobs above are filled from within along job ladders, with rules rather than open market prices.

Becker (1964)

General versus specific skill

Firms pay for skills that are worth more inside the firm than outside. Specific skill ties employer and employee together.

Prediction: where knowledge is firm specific, promoting from within should beat hiring from outside. Is that true?

L2 · The evidence on make versus buy

Bidwell (2011): paying more to get less

Pay

External hires into comparable jobs were paid about 18 percent more than internal promotees.

Performance

External hires received lower performance evaluations for their first two years.

Profile

They arrived with more education and experience, the very signals that justify the premium.

Exit

External hires were more likely to leave, so part of the premium was never recovered.

Bidwell, M. (2011). Administrative Science Quarterly, 56(3), 369 to 407

17

L2 · Why promotion motivates, and when it stops

Tournament theory

Lazear and Rosen (1981)

Promotion as prize

Pay is set by rank, not by output, so the gap between levels is a prize that motivates everyone below.

Lazear and Rosen (1981)

The convex structure

Prizes rise steeply near the top to keep effort high in later rounds of the contest.

Implication for the plateau

The cost

Those who judge themselves out of contention lose the incentive. A flattened structure removes the prize.

L2 · Talent on demand

Cappelli: treat talent like a supply chain

Make and buy

Develop internally where demand is predictable; buy from outside to cover uncertainty.

Small batches

Shorter development cycles reduce the cost of forecasting errors.

Share the cost

Let employees co invest in development to raise the return.

Balance interests

Internal mobility keeps the investment from walking out of the door.

Cappelli, P. (2008). Harvard Business Review, 86(3), 74 to 81; Cappelli (2008), Talent on Demand

20

L2 · How a move happens matters

Posting, slotting and the Peter principle

Keller (2018)

Posting beats slotting

Open internal posting produced better performing hires than managers slotting people they already knew.

Mechanism a marketplace targets

Talent hoarding

A manager who loses a strong performer bears the cost, so the manager's incentive works against mobility.

Benson, Li and Shue (2019)

The Peter principle

The best salespeople were most likely to be promoted, and their teams then performed worse.

Allen and Katz (1986)

Dual ladders

A technical ladder keeps experts without forcing management, but loses credibility if it becomes a consolation prize.

L2 · Beyond the ladder

Careers the person steers

Hall (2004)

Protean career

Driven by the person's own values, judged by psychological success rather than rank.

Arthur and Rousseau (1996)

Boundaryless career

Moves across firms, functions and networks; the firm is no longer the frame.

Benko and Weisberg (2007)

Lattice

Lateral and diagonal moves are legitimate progress, not detours.

Tension: the firm wants to retain specific skill; the protean employee wants portable growth. A mobility system is a settlement between the two.

L3 · Does mentoring work?

Eby, Allen, Evans, Ng and DuBois (2008)

The finding

Mentoring is associated with better behavioral, attitudinal, health, motivational and career outcomes.

The size

Most effects are small. Mentoring helps, but it is not the large lever programs often promise.

Small average effects suggest the active ingredient varies. Ibarra, Carter and Silva (2010) argue women received mentoring but less sponsorship.

Eby et al. (2008), Journal of Vocational Behavior, 72(2), 254 to 267; Ibarra, Carter and Silva (2010), Harvard Business Review

28

L3 · Why sponsorship works

A social capital theory of career success

Granovetter (1973)

Weak ties

Acquaintances bring information that close contacts do not already have.

Burt (1992)

Structural holes

People who bridge unconnected groups gain early information and control.

Seibert, Kraimer and Liden (2001)

The pathway

Network structure leads to access to information, resources and career sponsorship, which lead to salary, promotion and satisfaction.

A sponsor spends their own social capital on you. That is why sponsorship is scarce, and why it is unequally distributed.

L3 · The paradox of meritocracy

What happens when a firm says it rewards merit

Castilla (2008), American Journal of Sociology

Same rating, different raise

Employees with equal performance ratings received different salary increases by gender and race.

Castilla and Benard (2010), Administrative Science Quarterly

The paradox

In experiments, framing an organization as meritocratic led managers to favor men over equally performing women.

Castilla (2015), Organization Science

What reduced the gap

Making pay decisions transparent and making managers accountable for them.

Performance and potential
