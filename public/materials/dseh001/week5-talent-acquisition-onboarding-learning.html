PART A · THEORY 1

Hiring is a two way signaling problem

The employer reads the candidate

Spence (1973): when quality is hidden, employers rely on costly signals such as degrees and experience.

An ATS turns signals into keywords and cut offs, so it reads only what was written in its vocabulary.

The candidate reads the employer

Rynes, Bretz and Gerhart (1991): applicants treat delays, tone and process quality as signals of how the firm treats people.

A silent auto rejection is also a signal.

Design question: what does our ATS signal to a Tier 3 walk in applicant, and what can it not read about her?

PART A · THEORY 1b

A screen is only as useful as its validity

Taylor and Russell (1939)

A selection tool adds value through three things together:

validity (does the score predict performance?), the selection ratio and the base rate of good performers.

Meta analytic evidence

Schmidt and Hunter (1998); revised by Sackett et al. (2022).

Structured interviews rank among the most valid methods; unstructured ones rank lower.

Keyword screens are rarely validated at all.

Rigor test for any ATS: what is its validity against later job performance, and who measured it?

PART A · THEORY 2

The ASA cycle explains why screening models copy the past

Attraction

Similar people apply

→

Selection

Firm picks people like current staff

→

Attrition

Those who do not fit leave

→

Sameness

The workforce narrows

Schneider (1987)

“The people make the place”: attraction, selection and attrition together make an organization more alike over time.

Kristof (1996)

Person organization fit (values) differs from person job fit (skills). A model trained on past hires learns both, mixed.

PART A · THEORY 2b

Route (Barocas and Selbst, 2016)How it enters a GreenLeaf screen
1. Target variable“Good hire” defined as long tenure, which favors groups that faced fewer breaks
2. Labeled examplesPast offers, already skewed, used as the training answer key
3. Feature selectionFields chosen that exist for some applicants and not others
4. ProxiesCareer gap or college stands in for gender or class
5. MaskingA biased choice hidden inside a neutral looking model

PART B · THEORY 5

What onboarding must produce: three adjustment states

Inputs

Socialization tactics; the newcomer’s own information seeking

→

Adjustment

Role clarity
Self efficacy
Social acceptance

→

Outcomes

Performance, satisfaction, commitment, intention to stay

Measure the middle box. Completion of onboarding tasks is an input, not an outcome.

Bauer, Bodner, Erdogan, Truxillo and Tucker (2007), meta analysis, Journal of Applied Psychology, 92(3).

PART B · THEORY 5b

Newcomers reduce uncertainty by seeking information

Uncertainty reduction

Berger and Calabrese (1975): people in new relationships act to make others predictable.

Newcomers do the same with the organization.

Seeking tactics

Miller and Jablin (1991): overt questions, indirect questions, third parties, testing limits, observing, surveillance.

A chatbot serves only the first.

What they seek

Morrison (1993): technical, referent (role), normative, performance feedback, social.

Normative and social answers come from people.

Prediction: a digital first onboarding answers technical questions well and leaves normative and social uncertainty unresolved.

PART B · THEORY 5c

Onboarding writes the psychological contract

Rousseau (1989; 1995)

Beliefs about mutual promises, held by the employee.

Transactional: pay for work, short term.

Relational: loyalty, growth, long term.

Early breach

Robinson and Rousseau (1994): a majority of new graduates reported a broken promise within two years.

Breach predicted lower trust and higher exit.

Realistic previews

Wanous: met expectations. Premack and Wanous (1985) meta analysis: previews lower inflated expectations; effect on retention is small but reliable.

Design rule: every promise a recruiting app or welcome video makes is a term of the contract the first month must honor.

PART C · THEORY 8

How adults learn: two theories set the design brief

Knowles: andragogy (1980)

Adults need to know why.

They direct their own learning.

Experience is the main resource.

They learn around real problems.

Kolb: experiential cycle (1984)

1 Concrete experience

2 Reflective observation

3 Abstract conceptualization

4 Active experimentation

Caution: matching teaching to “learning styles” has no good evidence (Pashler et al., 2008).

PART C · THEORY 8b

Self determination theory: why points and badges backfire

Three basic needs

Deci and Ryan (1985); Ryan and Deci (2000).

Autonomy, competence and relatedness sustain intrinsic motivation.

Overjustification

Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999), meta analysis: expected tangible rewards undermined intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks.

Informational feedback did not.

Design consequences

Choice of path (autonomy).

Visible skill growth (competence).

Learning with peers (relatedness).

This is the theory behind the Round 3 caution: extrinsic rewards create activity without capability.

PART C · THEORY 9

Why micro and spaced: the forgetting curve

Three findings

Ebbinghaus (1885): memory fades fast without review.

Spacing effect: spread practice beats one long session (Cepeda et al., 2006).

Mayer: short segments, clear signals, no clutter.

The curve is illustrative, not data. Mayer, R. E. (2009). Multimedia Learning, 2nd ed. Cambridge University Press.

PART C · CRITIQUE

The four levels are not a causal chain

Alliger et al. (1997)

Meta analysis: reactions correlated only weakly with learning and with on the job behavior.

Judgments of usefulness did better than enjoyment.

Holton (1996)

The model is a taxonomy, not a theory: it names outcomes without explaining why one leads to another.

Context and motivation are left out.

So a high Level 2 score licenses no claim about Level 3. You must measure behavior directly, which GreenLeaf’s data allows.

PART C · THEORY 10b

Transfer of training: the missing causal model

Inputs

Trainee: ability, motivation
Design: practice, relevance
Work environment: support, chance to use

→

Learning

Learned and retained

→

Transfer

Used on the job and kept up

Blume et al. (2010) meta analysis: work environment support and motivation relate to transfer. A module cannot supply the store manager who lets you use what you learned.

Baldwin, T. T. and Ford, J. K. (1988). Transfer of training: A review and directions for future research. Personnel Psychology, 41(1).

THREE TAKEAWAYS

1

An ATS is a selection method: ask for its validity. The four fifths test detects the gap; ASA and Barocas and Selbst explain it.

2

Onboarding succeeds when adjustment rises: role clarity, self efficacy, acceptance. Design with a process framework, evaluate with an outcome model.

3

Completion is not transfer. Baldwin and Ford predict flat use without work support, and GreenLeaf’s data agrees, pending rival explanations.
